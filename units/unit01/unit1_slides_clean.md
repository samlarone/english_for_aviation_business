## 1. Understanding the industry

<p class="big">Aviation is the wider system. Airlines are one part of it.</p>

---

<span class="tag">Industry · 20 min</span>

## What is the aviation industry?

The **aviation industry** includes the businesses, organisations, and activities connected with aircraft and air transport.

It includes commercial and non-commercial aviation, aircraft manufacturing, airport services, maintenance, regulation, research, and more.

<!-- <img src="images/example.png" width="300"> -->
<!-- <img src="images/example.png" width="300"> -->

Note: Emphasise that aviation is broader than airlines. A company does not need to sell passenger tickets to be part of the aviation industry.


---

## What is the airline industry?

The **airline industry** consists of businesses that provide air transport services to passengers and/or cargo customers.

Airlines may operate:

<div class="cols">
<div class="card"><h3>Scheduled flights</h3>Flights operated according to a published timetable.</div>
<div class="card o"><h3>Charter flights</h3>Flights arranged for a particular customer, group, or purpose.</div>
</div>

Airlines are part of the wider aviation industry.

---

## Aviation industry vs. airline industry

<div class="cols">
<div class="card"><h3>Aviation industry</h3>The whole ecosystem.<br><br>Aircraft manufacturers, airports, airlines, maintenance providers, regulators, training organisations, and research companies.</div>
<div class="card o"><h3>Airline industry</h3>A specific sector.<br><br>Companies that transport passengers and/or cargo by air.</div>
</div>

**Remember:** Every airline is part of the aviation industry, but not every aviation business is an airline.

## 3. Reading: the aviation industry

<p class="big">Read for the main idea first. Then look for supporting details.</p>

---

<span class="tag">Reading · 25 min</span>

## Reading strategy: main idea and details

**Main idea** = the most important point of a paragraph or text.

**Supporting details** = facts, examples, explanations, and reasons that develop the main idea.

When reading, ask:

1. What is this paragraph mainly about?
2. Which sentence or detail supports that idea?
3. Are examples being used to explain a general point?

<div class="scroll">

## 4. Grammar: sentence structure

<p class="big">Clear aviation communication begins with clear sentences.</p>

---

<span class="tag">Grammar · 20 min</span>

## Every sentence needs a verb

A sentence normally gives a complete idea. A basic sentence has a **subject** and a **verb**. The subject tells us who or what the sentence is about. The verb tells us what the subject does or what happens to it.

<div class="cols">
<div class="card"><h3>Subject</h3>Who or what the sentence is about.</div>
<div class="card o"><h3>Verb</h3>The action or state.</div>
</div>

*The aircraft landed.*  ·  *Passengers are boarding.*  ·  *The airport operates 24 hours a day.*

## Parts of a sentence

| Part | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Who/what the sentence is about | **The pilot** called the tower. |
| Verb | Action or state | The pilot **called** the tower. |
| Object | Receives the action | The pilot called **the tower**. |
| Complement | Completes the meaning | The cabin is **comfortable**. |
| Adverbial | Adds information about time, place, manner, etc. | The aircraft landed **safely**. |

## 5. English and aviation safety

<p class="big">Clear communication is an operational requirement, not just a language skill.</p>

---

<span class="tag">Aviation English · 15 min</span>

## Why is English important in aviation?

English is widely used for international airport and airline communication.

Airport staff and flight attendants need to communicate clearly with passengers, especially when providing assistance, giving directions, and responding to unexpected situations.

<div class="cols">
<div class="card"><h3>Standard phraseology</h3>Uses agreed expressions for routine and many non-routine communications.</div>
<div class="card o"><h3>Plain language</h3>Helps communicate information when standard phraseology is not sufficient.</div>
</div>

---

## What can go wrong?

Miscommunication can lead to:

<div class="cols3">
<div class="card"><h3>Incorrect understanding</h3>A message is interpreted differently from its intended meaning.</div>
<div class="card"><h3>Delayed response</h3>Important information is not understood quickly enough.</div>
<div class="card o"><h3>Operational risk</h3>Decisions may be made using incomplete or incorrect information.</div>
</div>

Clear communication supports safety, coordination, and efficiency.

---

<span class="tag">ICAO · Rating scale</span>

## ICAO English language proficiency

The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) established language proficiency requirements for pilots and air traffic controllers involved in international radiotelephony communications.

The ICAO scale has six levels:

1. Pre-elementary
2. Elementary
3. Pre-operational
4. **Operational**
5. Extended
6. Expert

<span class="hl">Level 4</span> is the minimum required operational level for relevant radiotelephony communication.

## Reading: non-routine events

A pilot may be able to use standard phraseology for a non-routine event, but sometimes plain language is necessary.

A controller who usually handles domestic flights may have limited experience communicating in English with international pilots. A pilot should therefore use clear, simple, and precise language.

**Key lesson:** Do not use unnecessary words or several different expressions when one clear expression will communicate the message.

## Where do adverbs of frequency go?

**Before the main verb**

*Ground staff usually check passenger documents.*

**After the verb be**

*The airport is always busy.*

**Between an auxiliary and the main verb**

*The airline has never cancelled this route.*

They can also appear at the beginning or end of some sentences: *Sometimes, flights are delayed.*
